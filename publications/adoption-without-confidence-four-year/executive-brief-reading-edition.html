Favoring a tool. Doubting its answers.

THE AHAS / 2025 AND 2026

Attitude toward using AI and trust in its output are different answers. The 2025 results make that distinction concrete.

2025: 85.1% (205/241) of very favorable current users who highly distrusted AI accuracy reported daily use. Favorability and accuracy distrust coexisted in the same respondents.

Daily use can also coexist with disapproval.

In 2026, 54.3% of very unfavorable users reported daily use (520 of 958). The corresponding 2025 figure was 18.8% (110 of 585), under a different use question.

Daily in 2026 means daily on at least one of four tool types at work. These very unfavorable daily users were 4.8% of all daily users in the primary sample; most current users answering attitude were still favorable.

What those answers cannot tell us

The surveys do not establish whether these people checked outputs well, were compelled to use AI, or produced better work. Finding value in a tool while checking its answers is one possible explanation, not a demonstrated mechanism.

Primary samples: 26,102 current users in 2025; 14,158 in 2026, each with complete use, attitude and trust answers. The full whitepaper shows all attitude groups and uncertainty intervals, not only these endpoints.

The question changes the answer.

READING THE EVIDENCE

Attitude distinguished daily use in both years.

Within 2025 and within 2026, the attitude item ranked daily versus less-frequent users better than the survey trust item. The ordering held with respondent context included and in professional and country-grouped checks. Trust still improved the combined models.

Standalone ranking score (AUC): attitude/trust = 0.758/0.669 in 2025; 0.722/0.657 in 2026. AUC is not percent correct. The trust items differ. Favoring use is also conceptually closer to use frequency than trusting accuracy is; this is not proof that changing attitudes causes adoption.

The denominator changes the trust story.

In 2025, 32.8% of all accuracy-trust answerers (n = 33,297) trusted AI and 45.7% distrusted it. Among current-user answerers (n = 26,126), those shares were 39.3% and 37.3%: nearly balanced. A headline about everyone answering the question describes a different group from a headline about current users.

Daily frequency is not hours of use.

For four-plus-hours use in 2026, models using context plus attitude and context plus trust were essentially tied on AUC. The attitude advantage does not transfer uniformly to every outcome. No matching 2025 hours measure exists, so this is a boundary on the finding, not evidence of a reversal.

Keep five things separate

Use. How often and for which tasks.
Attitude. How people feel about using it.
Trust. What they will rely on and under what conditions.
Verification effort. Time and skill needed to check and correct.
Outcomes. Quality, total time and consequences in actual work.

Start with one piece of real work.

FROM FINDING TO PRACTICE

These are proposed practices informed by the findings, not interventions tested by the surveys.

  1. Establish a baseline.

    Name a workflow owner. Record output quality and total time, including checking and correction, before judging success.

  2. Watch the work happen.

    Walk through real tasks with favorable and unfavorable daily users. Ask what helps, what creates effort, and why they use AI.

  3. Make acceptance explicit.

    Define an acceptable result, required checks and who accepts the output. Track errors, rework and verification time.

  4. Make a decision and revisit it.

    Continue, redesign or stop that particular use. Record the evidence, the reason, an owner and a review date; repeat the measures.
